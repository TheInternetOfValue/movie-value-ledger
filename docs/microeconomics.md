# A proper frame for the microeconomics of film

At the micro level, a movie is not “GDP.” It is a **project-finance asset** inside a larger industrial system. The unit of analysis is the firm or consortium of firms: producer, studio, financier, distributor, exhibitor, streamer, broadcaster, music label, and sometimes franchise owner. The core micro question is simple: **who funded the film, who took the risk, who captured the rights, and who ended up with the surplus?**

That logic became much stronger over time because the industry itself changed. Early cinema was basically exhibition-led. Theatres and local distributors mattered most. Then the studio era consolidated production, talent, and distribution. Then TV and home video created second windows. Then global multiplexing and franchise filmmaking raised theatrical upside. Now streaming, licensing, and library monetization mean a big film is no longer one product. It is a **bundle of rights and cash-flow windows**. PwC’s 2025 outlook puts global cinema box office spending at about **US$33 billion in 2024**, rising toward **US$41.5 billion by 2029**, while the broader global entertainment and media industry is measured in the trillions, not billions. ([PwC][1])

That is why a modern studio is not just trying to “sell tickets.” It is trying to turn one title into a stack of monetization channels: theatrical, streaming, TV licensing, music, consumer products, and long-tail library value. Paramount’s filings show this clearly. In 2024, its Filmed Entertainment segment made about **10% of consolidated revenue**, and within that segment, **licensing and other revenues were about 72%**, while theatrical was about **27%**. That tells you something brutal and important: theatrical gets the headlines, but licensing often carries the heavier commercial weight. ([SEC][2])

# Global context: the firms and franchises that define the industry

If you want to compare the global industry properly, compare **integrated media-studio groups**, not just “production houses” in the narrow sense, because the biggest winners are firms that control both content and downstream monetization. Disney remains the clearest example. Its FY2025 annual reporting shows an enormous Entertainment segment, with operating income rising year over year, and its business is explicitly built around monetizing IP across theatrical, direct-to-consumer, and licensing. Warner Bros. Discovery reported **US$37.3 billion** in total 2025 revenue, with management explicitly calling the Studios segment a standout in 2025. Paramount, although smaller, is useful because it discloses filmed entertainment more transparently. ([investors.thewaltdisneycompany.com][3])

On the film side, the global benchmark titles still show what “franchise-scale microeconomics” looks like. The all-time global theatrical leaders remain titles like **Avatar**, **Avengers: Endgame**, **Avatar: The Way of Water**, and **Titanic**, each of which crossed into the multi-billion-dollar worldwide gross tier. The point is not fanboy trivia. The point is that these films are not merely successful releases. They are **balance-sheet events** for the companies behind them, because they amplify pricing power, sequel potential, downstream licensing, and bargaining strength with exhibitors and platforms. PwC’s 2025 commentary also notes that the top five US studios’ share has fallen compared with the pre-pandemic era, while local-language production has become stronger in many markets. So scale still matters, but local relevance matters more than before. ([PwC][4])

# India: one national market on paper, many industries in practice

India looks huge from the outside, but internally it behaves less like one movie industry and more like a federation of industries: Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam, Kannada, Bengali, Marathi, Punjabi, and others. EY says India’s film segment revenue **fell 5% to INR 187 billion in 2024**, with **more than 1,600 films released** that year. It also notes that both digital and satellite rights values fell around **10%**, and only **11 Hindi films** crossed the INR 1 billion gross mark in 2024. That tells you the Indian film business is both massive and harshly unequal. It releases a lot of films, but only a tiny share actually become high-margin corporate assets. ([EY][5])

The other Indian wrinkle is opacity. There is no fully standardized national public box-office system accepted by everyone. Rights values leak selectively. Producer recovery waterfalls are often unclear. Financing structures vary from formal studio capital to patchwork consortiums. That means any microeconomic model for an Indian title must distinguish clearly between **reported**, **estimated**, and **modeled** numbers. If you pretend the data is cleaner than it is, the report becomes decorative nonsense. The EY report and even high-grossing-film lists make this visible. They repeatedly note the dependence on estimates and the unevenness of the market. ([EY][5])

Still, India’s scale is real. IBEF notes India remains one of the world’s largest film markets by admissions, and reported that more than **157 million Indians bought 943 million movie tickets in 2023**. So the country has enormous volume, but the microeconomic spoils are captured by a relatively small number of event films and companies. ([India Brand Equity Foundation][6])

# Where Dhurandhar sits in that hierarchy

This is where the micro case becomes interesting. **Dhurandhar** is not just another successful Hindi release. On current tallies and trade estimates, it is already in the very top tier of Indian theatrical performers. Public high-grossing lists place **Dhurandhar** around **₹1,350.83 crore to ₹1,428 crore** worldwide, putting it among the biggest Indian films ever and among the very top Hindi titles. The sequel, **Dhurandhar: The Revenge**, opened extremely strongly and is already treated by public trackers as a major 2026 Hindi release, though its final run is still evolving. ([Wikipedia][7])

The corporate structure matters just as much as the gross. Public credits for both films show **Jio Studios** and **B62 Studios** as the studio entities, with **Jyoti Deshpande, Aditya Dhar, and Lokesh Dhar** as producers. That means the franchise is best read as a partnership between a **creative-originating producer banner** and a **large integrated media capital platform**. B62 matters because it is the IP and project-assembly node. Jio matters because it brings funding capacity, marketing firepower, distribution leverage, and strategic value inside a broader media ecosystem. ([Wikipedia][8])

That makes Dhurandhar more than a box-office phenomenon. It becomes a **rights-stack and power-stack event**. Reliance’s annual reporting emphasizes that its media business spans film, streaming, television, and sports. So a Jio-backed hit has value not only as cash flow from one film, but as a strategic asset inside a bigger content-distribution machine. That is a very different microeconomic reality from a one-film independent producer trying to recover costs from ticket sales alone. ([EY][9])

# The right micro model for Dhurandhar

A serious micro model should not start with box office and stop there. It should start with the full revenue stack and the full cost stack.

At the franchise level, the revenue identity is:

[
TR = BO_{India\ producer\ share} + BO_{Overseas\ realization} + OTT + Satellite + Music + Ancillary
]

The cost identity is:

[
TC = Production + P&A + Distribution + Financing + Talent\ premium + Overheads
]

And the simplest project-level enterprise result is:

[
Project\ EBITDA = TR - TC_{cash\ operating}
]

Then:

[
EBIT = EBITDA - Depreciation/Amortization
]

[
PBT = EBIT - Interest
]

[
PAT = PBT - Tax
]

For film projects, D&A can be stylized or folded into amortization of production costs depending on reporting treatment, but the key is that **interest and taxes must be shown separately** if you are framing it like a DCF-lite or project-finance memo.

# Dhurandhar franchise assumptions for a combined model

You asked to model the franchise as **Part 1 actual-ish plus Part 2 at 1.5x Part 1 revenue**. That assumption should be explicit, not buried.

So the clean combined base-case assumption is:

[
WWG_{P2} = 1.5 \times WWG_{P1}
]

Using a central Part 1 worldwide-gross anchor of roughly **₹1,353 crore**, Part 2 becomes:

[
WWG_{P2} = 1.5 \times 1353 = 2029.5\ \text{crore}
]

So the combined franchise worldwide gross becomes:

[
WWG_{Franchise} = 1353 + 2029.5 = 3382.5\ \text{crore}
]

That is the gross-level theatrical headline, not the producer’s retained revenue.

To get to firm economics, you need to convert theatrical gross into producer-distributor realization. A reasonable India base-case for a giant event film is to assume about **50.5% of India net** accrues to producer-distributor pools, and about **42% of overseas gross** is effectively realized back to producer-distributor pools after downstream sharing. That is modeled, not audited. Based on the earlier macro work, a combined India net around **₹2,300 crore** and overseas gross around **₹660 crore** gives:

[
PD_{India} \approx 0.505 \times 2300.25 = 1161.63
]

[
PD_{Overseas} \approx 0.42 \times 660.31 = 277.33
]

Then add non-theatrical rights. Earlier working assumptions used around **₹380 crore** combined for rights, but given the scale of this franchise and the strength of Jio-backed packaging, a more ambitious but still defensible base case for combined OTT, satellite, music, and ancillary rights could sit in a broader range of **₹450 crore to ₹700 crore**. To stay conservative but not timid, I would use **₹550 crore** as a base-case combined rights stack.

That gives combined top-line producer/studio revenue of roughly:

[
TR \approx 1161.63 + 277.33 + 550 = 1988.96\ \text{crore}
]

Round that to about **₹1,990 crore** base-case project revenue to the producer-studio-distribution side.

# Cost side for the combined franchise

Now the cost stack. Public material around the Dhurandhar duology points to a **combined budget in the ₹250 crore to ₹475 crore range** across both parts, though those public ranges are noisy. For a serious micro model, it is better to define the all-in project cost stack rather than blindly trust one headline budget number.

A realistic high-event-film combined cost stack could be framed as:

| Cost block                             |  Base case |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------: |
| Production                             | ₹380 crore |
| P&A / marketing                        |  ₹90 crore |
| Distribution / release logistics       |  ₹20 crore |
| Talent premium and backend equivalents |  ₹80 crore |
| Corporate overhead / contingency       |  ₹25 crore |

That gives a cash operating cost base of:

[
TC_{operating} = 380 + 90 + 20 + 80 + 25 = 595\ \text{crore}
]

Now financing. If 30% of that base is debt-funded:

[
Debt = 0.30 \times 595 = 178.5
]

At a 10% financing cost:

[
Interest = 0.10 \times 178.5 = 17.85
]

Assume straight-line amortization/depreciation of capitalized production and release assets is embedded through the life of the project at, say, **₹25 crore** for analytical clarity.

Then EBITDA becomes:

[
EBITDA = 1988.96 - 595 = 1393.96
]

EBIT becomes:

[
EBIT = 1393.96 - 25 = 1368.96
]

PBT becomes:

[
PBT = 1368.96 - 17.85 = 1351.11
]

At a 25% effective tax rate:

[
Tax = 0.25 \times 1351.11 = 337.78
]

Then PAT becomes:

[
PAT = 1351.11 - 337.78 = 1013.33
]

So the combined franchise, under this base-case model, can be framed as approximately:

| Metric                  |    Base case |
| ----------------------- | -----------: |
| Producer/studio revenue | ₹1,989 crore |
| Operating cost          |   ₹595 crore |
| EBITDA                  | ₹1,394 crore |
| EBIT                    | ₹1,369 crore |
| PBT                     | ₹1,351 crore |
| PAT                     | ₹1,013 crore |

Now, would I present **₹1,013 crore PAT** as a hard fact? No. That would be stupid. I would present it as a **modeled base case** under the stated assumptions. But this is already a far more adult treatment than “box office = profit.”

# What this means strategically

At the micro level, Dhurandhar is valuable for three reasons.

First, it likely generates a substantial direct surplus pool for the producer-studio stack. Even if you haircut the rights value and theatrical realization assumptions, the franchise still looks like a very profitable corporate asset under a strong-run scenario.

Second, it increases bargaining power. A banner like **B62 Studios** gains reputational leverage, sequel leverage, and much easier access to future capital. This matters in India because financing friction is real and trust is uneven.

Third, **Jio Studios** gains more than cash. It gains a stronger content library, stronger distribution relevance, and stronger negotiating position inside a broader Reliance media ecosystem. That strategic value is real even when it does not show up neatly in a single-film P&L.
